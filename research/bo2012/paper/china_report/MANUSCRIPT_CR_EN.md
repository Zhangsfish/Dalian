# Political Network Shocks and Local Economic Vulnerability: Evidence from Dalian after 2012

## Abstract

**Purpose:** This article examines why the 2012 collapse of a major political network was followed by a sharp economic slowdown in Dalian but not by a comparable downturn in Chongqing.

**Approach:** It combines city-level growth comparisons, a nested Dalian–Liaoning comparison, sectoral and financial data, evidence on fiscal and project resources, and Chongqing as a parallel exposed case.

**Findings:** Dalian’s growth gap relative to Wuhan and Qingdao deteriorated by about 4.6 percentage points between 2008–2011 and 2013–2015, while its deterioration relative to Liaoning was only about 1.5 points. The regional slowdown coincided with collapsing real-estate and fixed-asset investment. Within Dalian, secondary-industry growth, industrial revenues and profits weakened sharply, non-performing loans rose, and bank credit slowed, even as fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and large projects continued. Chongqing did not display a similar investment or credit collapse after the same political shock.

**Conclusion:** Political network shocks do not translate mechanically into local economic decline. Their macroeconomic consequences depend on the structural vulnerability of local investment regimes, corporate cash flow and credit systems.

**Keywords:** political networks; local economic vulnerability; Dalian; Liaoning; credit contraction; local political economy

## 1. Introduction

Political connections are widely understood to matter in China because local development depends not only on formal policy rules but also on coordination across governments, banks, state-owned firms and project authorities. A large literature shows that cadre incentives and political ties can shape promotion, policy choices and the distribution of public resources (Li and Zhou 2005; Jia, Kudamatsu, and Seim 2015; Persson and Zhuravskaya 2016; Jiang and Zhang 2020). A harder question is what happens when the value of a long-standing political network suddenly changes. Does a local economy lose growth simply because an elite patron falls, or do political ties matter mainly when they interact with an already fragile local growth model?

Dalian provides an unusually revealing case. Bo Xilai spent much of his local political career in Dalian, serving first as acting mayor, then mayor and later municipal party secretary before moving to higher provincial and national office. His political downfall in 2012 therefore offered an intuitive explanation for Dalian's subsequent slowdown: a city that had benefited from a powerful political network may have lost access to projects, credit and higher-level coordination when that network was abruptly devalued. The timing is superficially persuasive. Dalian's real GDP growth fell from 9.0 per cent in 2013 to 5.8 per cent in 2014 and 4.2 per cent in 2015.

Yet the same years were also a turning point for Liaoning and the wider Northeast. Real-estate investment, fixed-asset investment and traditional industrial activity weakened sharply across the province. Dalian was therefore exposed simultaneously to a political shock, a regional investment downturn and a structural transition in an economy with substantial heavy-industry, construction and external-manufacturing exposure. Treating all post-2012 weakness as a political effect would collapse these distinct processes into a single story.

This article instead asks why the 2012 political shock was followed by a pronounced slowdown in Dalian but not by a comparable macroeconomic break in Chongqing, where Bo Xilai was serving as party secretary when the political crisis occurred. The empirical strategy is deliberately layered. First, Dalian is compared with Wuhan and Qingdao to establish whether it experienced an unusual slowdown relative to large cities outside Liaoning. Second, Dalian is compared with Liaoning to locate how much of that divergence occurred at the regional rather than city-specific level. Third, sectoral, investment and financial data are used to trace the transition from weakening real-estate and fixed investment to industrial cash-flow stress, non-performing loans and slower bank credit. Finally, fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and major projects are examined alongside Chongqing to distinguish a general loss of formal public resources from a more conditional weakening of marginal finance and coordination.

Three findings stand out. First, Dalian clearly lost ground relative to external comparison cities. Its average real-GDP growth advantage over an equal-weighted Wuhan–Qingdao benchmark shifted from +1.80 percentage points in 2008–2011 to −2.77 points in 2013–2015, a reversal of roughly 4.6 percentage points. But Dalian's deterioration relative to Liaoning over the same windows was only about 1.5 points. The larger part of the external divergence therefore coincided with a broader Liaoning slowdown rather than an exclusively Dalian-specific break.

Second, the aggregate growth numbers conceal a much sharper deterioration in capital-intensive activity. Dalian's secondary-industry growth fell from 9.4 per cent in 2013 to 0.9 per cent in 2015, while tertiary-industry growth remained at 8.2 per cent. Over the same period, revenue and profits among above-scale industrial firms moved from positive growth to declines of 27.4 and 30.7 per cent respectively, and construction output also contracted sharply. As firm cash flow and project returns deteriorated, the non-performing-loan ratio rose and bank-credit growth increasingly lagged Liaoning. The financial weakness thus appears less as an isolated banking event than as part of a feedback loop linking the regional investment downturn to local balance sheets and new investment.

Third, formal public resources did not disappear in parallel with bank credit. Comparable central and provincial fiscal transfers to Dalian increased between 2014 and 2015, national-level policy platforms continued to be approved, and major industrial projects still entered the city. Chongqing also failed to reproduce Dalian's investment and credit collapse after the same political episode. These patterns are difficult to reconcile with a simple account in which the fall of a political patron mechanically removes a city's access to development resources.

The article therefore advances a conditional interpretation of political connections. Political networks can matter through project coordination, refinancing, risk assessment and access to marginal credit, but their macroeconomic consequences depend on the health of the local investment regime on which those channels operate. Where industrial cash flow, property investment and asset quality remain strong, a political shock need not generate a visible economic break. Where those fundamentals are already deteriorating, the same loss of coordination capacity can amplify adjustment.

This argument contributes to the study of Chinese local political economy in three ways. It shifts attention from whether political connections matter to the conditions under which their loss becomes economically consequential. It distinguishes visible public resources from less visible coordination and credit channels, showing that formal transfers and national projects can remain available even as marginal financing weakens. Finally, by placing Dalian alongside Liaoning and Chongqing, it treats political shocks as interacting with subnational growth regimes rather than as stand-alone explanations for local performance. The next section develops this framework before the article turns to the 2012 event, the comparative design and the empirical evidence.

## 2. Political Networks and Local Economic Vulnerability in China

China's local governments have long occupied an unusually active position in economic development. Classic accounts of local state corporatism emphasised the incentives of local officials to organise production, mobilise resources and coordinate firms (Oi 1992), while work on local governments as industrial organisations highlighted the close relationship between jurisdictional incentives and enterprise performance (Walder 1995). More broadly, China's reform system has combined central control over political personnel with extensive subnational responsibility for implementing policy and organising economic activity (Xu 2011). These institutional features make local growth particularly sensitive to both the formal authority and the informal coordinating capacity of local political actors.

Political networks operate within this system in several ways. Personnel incentives link economic performance to political careers (Li and Zhou 2005), while political connections and performance jointly shape elite advancement (Jia, Kudamatsu, and Seim 2015). Local ties also influence policy choices and the behaviour of officials (Persson and Zhuravskaya 2016). Most directly relevant here, Jiang and Zhang (2020) show that patronage networks can affect the distribution of fiscal transfers and facilitate policy coordination. Political connections therefore have an economic value not because a single politician personally allocates every loan or project, but because networks can reduce coordination costs across agencies and improve access to information, approvals and higher-level support.

For local development, however, it is useful to distinguish two forms of political-network value. The first is **visible resource allocation**: fiscal transfers, national policy designations, major state projects and other resources that can be observed directly. If the loss of a powerful political connection primarily works through this channel, a political shock should be followed by a visible decline in these flows or in the city's ability to obtain new policy platforms.

The second is **marginal coordination**. Local governments do not directly determine all commercial lending, but they influence the environment in which projects are screened, land and collateral are arranged, state firms and platforms are coordinated, and troubled borrowers seek refinancing. When project profitability is high and balance sheets are healthy, many investments can proceed without extraordinary political coordination. When cash flow deteriorates and uncertainty rises, the value of such coordination becomes larger because more projects sit near the margin between renewal and retrenchment. A political-network shock may therefore matter most through changes in risk assessment, refinancing, project continuation and private expectations rather than through an immediate disappearance of aggregate funding.

This distinction leads to the article's central concept of **local economic vulnerability**. The same political shock can have different economic consequences depending on the condition of the local growth regime. In an investment-led economy, weakening property demand or industrial profitability can reduce project returns, erode collateral values and increase credit risk. Banks respond more cautiously, firms cut borrowing and investment, and the resulting decline in new activity further weakens cash flow. Under these conditions, any loss of political coordination can amplify an adjustment already under way. By contrast, where investment opportunities remain strong and credit continues to expand, the same political shock may have little visible macroeconomic effect.

Dalian is particularly useful for examining this interaction because several potential mechanisms overlap. One is the broader Liaoning and Northeast investment cycle. If Dalian's slowdown mainly reflects a regional real-estate, heavy-industry and fixed-investment downturn, its investment path should closely resemble Liaoning's and its deterioration relative to the province should be much smaller than its deterioration relative to external cities. A second mechanism is Dalian's specific exposure to Japanese manufacturing, trade and business networks. A gradual decline in new Japanese projects could weaken a long-standing source of incremental growth, but it would be less convincing as an explanation for a sudden 2014 break if actual investment and incumbent activity remained substantial. A third mechanism is the interaction between corporate cash-flow stress and credit risk. If this channel is important, weakening industrial revenue and profits should precede or accompany rising non-performing loans, slower bank credit and weaker private investment.

These mechanisms generate different empirical signatures. A broad loss of formal political access should appear in fiscal transfers, national policy designations and major projects. A regional investment downturn should appear simultaneously in Dalian and Liaoning. A Dalian-specific financial amplification should appear in credit, asset quality and private investment relative to the province. A slow-moving external-structure explanation should evolve more gradually than the 2014–2015 break. The article therefore does not rely on a single post-2012 coefficient to separate these explanations. It compares where, when and through which channels the deterioration appears.

The comparison with Chongqing provides an additional test of the conditional argument. Chongqing was more directly exposed to the 2012 political event because Bo Xilai was its party secretary at the time. If the political shock itself were sufficient to generate a local economic collapse, Chongqing should display a broadly comparable post-event pattern. If, instead, the macroeconomic effect of political networks depends on the underlying investment and credit regime, the two cities can diverge despite exposure to the same elite political rupture. This expectation motivates the comparative evidence developed below.
