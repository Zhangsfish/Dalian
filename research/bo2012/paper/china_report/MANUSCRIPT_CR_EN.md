# Political Network Shocks and Local Economic Vulnerability: Evidence from Dalian after 2012

## Abstract

**Purpose:** This article examines why the 2012 political downfall of Bo Xilai, which abruptly altered the value of long-standing political ties, was followed by a sharp economic slowdown in Dalian but not by a comparable downturn in Chongqing.

**Approach:** It combines city-level growth comparisons, a nested Dalian–Liaoning comparison, sectoral and financial data, evidence on fiscal and project resources, and Chongqing as a parallel exposed case.

**Findings:** Dalian’s growth gap relative to Wuhan and Qingdao deteriorated by about 4.6 percentage points between 2008–2011 and 2013–2015, while its deterioration relative to Liaoning was only about 1.5 points. The regional slowdown coincided with collapsing real-estate and fixed-asset investment. Within Dalian, secondary-industry growth, industrial revenues and profits weakened sharply, non-performing loans rose, and bank credit slowed, even as fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and large projects continued. Chongqing did not display a similar investment or credit collapse despite being more directly exposed to the 2012 elite political rupture.

**Conclusion:** Political network shocks do not translate mechanically into local economic decline. Their macroeconomic consequences depend on the structural vulnerability of local investment regimes, corporate cash flow and credit systems.

**Keywords:** political networks; local economic vulnerability; Dalian; Liaoning; credit contraction; local political economy

---

## 1. Introduction

Political connections are widely understood to matter in China because local development depends not only on formal policy rules but also on coordination across governments, banks, state-owned firms and project authorities. A large literature shows that cadre incentives and political ties can shape promotion, policy choices and the distribution of public resources (Li and Zhou 2005; Jia, Kudamatsu, and Seim 2015; Persson and Zhuravskaya 2016; Jiang and Zhang 2020). A harder question is what happens when the value of a long-standing political network suddenly changes. Does a local economy lose growth simply because an elite patron falls, or do political ties matter mainly when they interact with an already fragile local growth model?

Dalian provides an unusually revealing case. Bo Xilai spent much of his local political career in Dalian, serving first as acting mayor, then mayor and later municipal party secretary before moving to higher provincial and national office. His political downfall in 2012 therefore offered an intuitive explanation for Dalian's subsequent slowdown: a city that had benefited from a powerful political network may have lost access to projects, credit and higher-level coordination when that network was abruptly devalued. The timing is superficially persuasive. Dalian's real GDP growth fell from 9.0 per cent in 2013 to 5.8 per cent in 2014 and 4.2 per cent in 2015.

Yet the same years were also a turning point for Liaoning and the wider Northeast. Real-estate investment, fixed-asset investment and traditional industrial activity weakened sharply across the province. Dalian was therefore exposed simultaneously to a political shock, a regional investment downturn and a structural transition in an economy with substantial heavy-industry, construction and external-manufacturing exposure. Treating all post-2012 weakness as a political effect would collapse these distinct processes into a single story.

This article instead asks why the 2012 political shock was followed by a pronounced slowdown in Dalian but not by a comparable macroeconomic break in Chongqing, where Bo Xilai was serving as party secretary when the political crisis occurred. The empirical strategy is deliberately layered. First, Dalian is compared with Wuhan and Qingdao to establish whether it experienced an unusual slowdown relative to large cities outside Liaoning. Second, Dalian is compared with Liaoning to locate how much of that divergence occurred at the regional rather than city-specific level. Third, sectoral, investment and financial data are used to trace the transition from weakening real-estate and fixed investment to industrial cash-flow stress, non-performing loans and slower bank credit. Finally, fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and major projects are examined alongside Chongqing to distinguish a general loss of formal public resources from a more conditional weakening of marginal finance and coordination.

Three findings stand out. First, Dalian clearly lost ground relative to external comparison cities. Its average real-GDP growth advantage over an equal-weighted Wuhan–Qingdao benchmark shifted from +1.80 percentage points in 2008–2011 to −2.77 points in 2013–2015, a reversal of roughly 4.6 percentage points. But Dalian's deterioration relative to Liaoning over the same windows was only about 1.5 points. The larger part of the external divergence therefore coincided with a broader Liaoning slowdown rather than an exclusively Dalian-specific break.

Second, the aggregate growth numbers conceal a much sharper deterioration in capital-intensive activity. Dalian's secondary-industry growth fell from 9.4 per cent in 2013 to 0.9 per cent in 2015, while tertiary-industry growth remained at 8.2 per cent. Over the same period, revenue and profits among above-scale industrial firms moved from positive growth to declines of 27.4 and 30.7 per cent respectively, and construction output also contracted sharply. As firm cash flow and project returns deteriorated, the non-performing-loan ratio rose and bank-credit growth increasingly lagged Liaoning. The financial weakness thus appears less as an isolated banking event than as part of a feedback loop linking the regional investment downturn to local balance sheets and new investment.

Third, formal public resources did not disappear in parallel with bank credit. Comparable central and provincial fiscal transfers to Dalian increased between 2014 and 2015, national-level policy platforms continued to be approved, and major industrial projects still entered the city. Chongqing also failed to reproduce Dalian's investment and credit collapse despite being more directly exposed to the 2012 political episode. These patterns are difficult to reconcile with a simple account in which the fall of a political patron mechanically removes a city's access to development resources.

The article therefore advances a conditional interpretation of political connections. Political networks can matter through project coordination, refinancing, risk assessment and access to marginal credit, but their macroeconomic consequences depend on the health of the local investment regime on which those channels operate. Where industrial cash flow, property investment and asset quality remain strong, a political shock need not generate a visible economic break. Where those fundamentals are already deteriorating, the same loss of coordination capacity can amplify adjustment.

This argument contributes to the study of Chinese local political economy in three ways. It shifts attention from whether political connections matter to the conditions under which their loss becomes economically consequential. It distinguishes visible public resources from less visible coordination and credit channels, showing that formal transfers and national projects can remain available even as marginal financing weakens. Finally, by placing Dalian alongside Liaoning and Chongqing, it treats political shocks as interacting with subnational growth regimes rather than as stand-alone explanations for local performance. The next section develops this framework before the article turns to the 2012 event, the comparative design and the empirical evidence.

## 2. Political Networks and Local Economic Vulnerability in China

China's local governments have long occupied an unusually active position in economic development. Classic accounts of local state corporatism emphasised the incentives of local officials to organise production, mobilise resources and coordinate firms (Oi 1992), while work on local governments as industrial organisations highlighted the close relationship between jurisdictional incentives and enterprise performance (Walder 1995). More broadly, China's reform system has combined central control over political personnel with extensive subnational responsibility for implementing policy and organising economic activity (Xu 2011). These institutional features make local growth particularly sensitive to both the formal authority and the informal coordinating capacity of local political actors.

Political networks operate within this system in several ways. Personnel incentives link economic performance to political careers (Li and Zhou 2005), while political connections and performance jointly shape elite advancement (Jia, Kudamatsu, and Seim 2015). Local ties also influence policy choices and the behaviour of officials (Persson and Zhuravskaya 2016). Most directly relevant here, Jiang and Zhang (2020) show that patronage networks can affect the distribution of fiscal transfers and facilitate policy coordination. Political connections therefore have an economic value not because a single politician personally allocates every loan or project, but because networks can reduce coordination costs across agencies and improve access to information, approvals and higher-level support. The financial channel is especially relevant. Cai, Xu, and Zeng (2017) find that political pressure on provincial leaders is associated with stronger bank-loan growth and lower loan quality, particularly in state-owned banks. Ding et al. (2023) show that when Chinese firms lost politically connected independent directors after a 2013 regulatory change, firms without compensating social ties to banks experienced a substantial decline in bank-loan ratios. Together, these studies make it plausible that the value of political connections can become visible at the financing margin when lenders reassess risk.

For local development, however, it is useful to distinguish two forms of political-network value. The first is **visible resource allocation**: fiscal transfers, national policy designations, major state projects and other resources that can be observed directly. If the loss of a powerful political connection primarily works through this channel, a political shock should be followed by a visible decline in these flows or in the city's ability to obtain new policy platforms.

The second is **marginal coordination**. Local governments do not directly determine all commercial lending, but they influence the environment in which projects are screened, land and collateral are arranged, state firms and platforms are coordinated, and troubled borrowers seek refinancing. When project profitability is high and balance sheets are healthy, many investments can proceed without extraordinary political coordination. When cash flow deteriorates and uncertainty rises, the value of such coordination becomes larger because more projects sit near the margin between renewal and retrenchment. A political-network shock may therefore matter most through changes in risk assessment, refinancing, project continuation and private expectations rather than through an immediate disappearance of aggregate funding.

This distinction leads to the article's central concept of **local economic vulnerability**. The same political shock can have different economic consequences depending on the condition of the local growth regime. In an investment-led economy, weakening property demand or industrial profitability can reduce project returns, erode collateral values and increase credit risk. Banks respond more cautiously, firms cut borrowing and investment, and the resulting decline in new activity further weakens cash flow. Under these conditions, any loss of political coordination can amplify an adjustment already under way. By contrast, where investment opportunities remain strong and credit continues to expand, the same political shock may have little visible macroeconomic effect.

Dalian is particularly useful for examining this interaction because several potential mechanisms overlap. One is the broader Liaoning and Northeast investment cycle. If Dalian's slowdown mainly reflects a regional real-estate, heavy-industry and fixed-investment downturn, its investment path should closely resemble Liaoning's and its deterioration relative to the province should be much smaller than its deterioration relative to external cities. A second mechanism is Dalian's specific exposure to Japanese manufacturing, trade and business networks. A gradual decline in new Japanese projects could weaken a long-standing source of incremental growth, but it would be less convincing as an explanation for a sudden 2014 break if actual investment and incumbent activity remained substantial. A third mechanism is the interaction between corporate cash-flow stress and credit risk. If this channel is important, weakening industrial revenue and profits should precede or accompany rising non-performing loans, slower bank credit and weaker private investment.

These mechanisms generate different empirical signatures. A broad loss of formal political access should appear in fiscal transfers, national policy designations and major projects. A regional investment downturn should appear simultaneously in Dalian and Liaoning. A Dalian-specific financial amplification should appear in credit, asset quality and private investment relative to the province. A slow-moving external-structure explanation should evolve more gradually than the 2014–2015 break. The article therefore does not rely on a single post-2012 coefficient to separate these explanations. It compares where, when and through which channels the deterioration appears.

The comparison with Chongqing provides an additional test of the conditional argument. Chongqing was more directly exposed to the 2012 political event because Bo Xilai was its party secretary at the time. If the political shock itself were sufficient to generate a local economic collapse, Chongqing should display a broadly comparable post-event pattern. If, instead, the macroeconomic effect of political networks depends on the underlying investment and credit regime, the two cities can diverge despite exposure to the same elite political rupture. This expectation motivates the comparative evidence developed below.

## 3. Dalian, the 2012 Shock, and Research Design

### 3.1 The political event and Dalian's historical connection

On 15 March 2012, China's central authorities announced that Bo Xilai would no longer serve as party secretary of Chongqing (Xinhua News Agency 2012). His political position subsequently deteriorated further. The event mattered for Dalian because Bo's relationship with the city was unusually long. He served as mayor of Dalian from 1993 and became municipal party secretary in 1999 before moving to provincial and national positions (People's Daily Online 2013). The relevance of his Dalian relationships did not end with his municipal tenure. The 2013 court judgment documented dealings with Dalian International and Shide Group that extended into his later provincial and national posts, while benefits from Shide chairman Xu Ming to Bo's family continued through 2012 (People's Daily Online 2013). During his Dalian years, the city pursued an activist development strategy centred on urban construction, development zones, investment promotion and large projects; Bo himself publicly described urban governance in terms of “managing the city” as a valuable public asset (People's Daily 2001).

The 2012 event is therefore treated here as a sudden shock to the perceived value of a long-standing political network, not as a simple policy intervention. The court evidence establishes that some Dalian-based relationships persisted beyond Bo's municipal tenure, but it does not establish that a single, measurable Dalian patronage network remained intact in 2012. Political networks are not directly observable binary treatments. They consist of relationships, information channels, reputational effects and coordinating capacity that may persist after an individual politician leaves office and may be partially replaced over time. The empirical question is consequently not whether every post-2012 change can be assigned to the event, but whether the spatial and temporal pattern of economic change is consistent with the channels through which such a network could plausibly matter.

### 3.2 Data and comparison strategy

The analysis combines annual city- and provincial-level macroeconomic data from official statistical bulletins, local fiscal reports, People's Bank of China financial materials, State Council and National Development and Reform Commission policy documents, and JETRO reports on Japanese investment in Dalian. The main growth series use real-GDP growth rates as reported in contemporaneous annual bulletins. This choice avoids mixing revised historical levels from different statistical vintages. Historical revisions are important in Chinese local data, particularly around economic censuses and later moves toward unified accounting, so nominal historical GDP levels are not used to construct the main post-2012 counterfactual.

The primary external comparison uses Wuhan and Qingdao. Wuhan provides a large regional-centre benchmark with strong industrial and service activity, while Qingdao provides a coastal port and open industrial city with characteristics closer to Dalian's external orientation. Neither city is embedded in Liaoning's regional property, industrial and fiscal cycle. The choice is substantive rather than algorithmic: Wuhan and Qingdao are complementary external benchmarks, not matched untreated controls. The article therefore begins with the annual difference between Dalian's real-GDP growth rate and their equal-weighted average; the supplementary material shows that the main reversal remains when either city is used alone and when the pre-event relative trend is extrapolated. The main pre-event period is 2008–2011. The year 2012 is treated as a transition year because the political event occurred in March and annual data cannot cleanly separate pre- and post-event months. The primary post-event window is 2013–2015, allowing time for investment, credit and project channels to adjust.

A second comparison places Dalian inside its regional context. Dalian is part of Liaoning, and the province's economy contains many of the same heavy-industry, property and fixed-investment exposures. The Dalian–Liaoning comparison is therefore not a conventional untreated control design. Liaoning also mechanically contains Dalian in the provincial aggregate. Its purpose is narrower and more useful for this article: to determine whether the slowdown is mainly a Dalian-specific break or part of a broader regional deterioration. If Dalian loses roughly the same amount of growth momentum as the rest of Liaoning, the case for a city-specific political explanation becomes weaker. If Dalian deteriorates much more than the province, the need for an additional city-specific mechanism becomes stronger.

The analysis then follows the slowdown through several outcomes rather than relying on GDP alone. Real-estate and fixed-asset investment capture the regional investment regime. Secondary-industry growth, industrial revenue, industrial profit and construction output show whether the aggregate slowdown reaches the cash flows of capital-intensive firms. Bank-loan growth, non-performing loans and private investment capture financial amplification. Fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and major projects indicate whether visible public resources disappear at the same time. Japanese investment and new-project entry provide a slower-moving alternative explanation linked to Dalian's distinctive external economic structure.

Finally, Chongqing is used as a parallel exposed case rather than as part of Dalian's donor pool. Bo Xilai was serving as Chongqing party secretary when the 2012 event occurred, making Chongqing more directly exposed to the immediate political rupture. Yet Chongqing had a different administrative status, industrial structure and investment cycle. The comparison therefore asks whether a similar political shock produces a similar macroeconomic trajectory when the underlying local growth regime differs.

### 3.3 Interpretation and inference

The principal statistics are changes in relative growth gaps before and after 2012, annual trajectories and cross-outcome timing. With one core case and a small number of comparison cities, conventional large-sample inference is not especially informative (Conley and Taber 2011; Ferman and Pinto 2019). The article therefore places more weight on whether the result survives alternative external benchmarks, whether the timing of the break is visible in annual data, and whether independent mechanism variables move at the same spatial level.

The supplementary material reports Wuhan-only and Qingdao-only comparisons, alternative windows, pre-trend extrapolation, data-vintage checks and synthetic-control feasibility diagnostics. Synthetic control is not used as the main causal estimator because historical city GDP series are affected by non-synchronous statistical revisions (Abadie, Diamond, and Hainmueller 2010; Abadie 2021). The main text instead uses transparent comparisons whose assumptions and limitations are directly visible to the reader.

This design supports three distinct levels of inference. It can establish whether Dalian slowed relative to relevant external cities; it can locate how much of that slowdown coincided with a wider Liaoning decline; and it can identify which economic channels changed in ways consistent with regional transmission or local amplification. It cannot, on aggregate annual data alone, assign a precise treatment effect to the loss of political-network value. The article's contribution lies in narrowing the plausible mechanisms and showing how political and economic conditions interacted.

## 4. Regional Slowdown and Dalian's Relative Decline

### 4.1 The external growth reversal

Dalian's post-2012 slowdown is large enough to require explanation. Between 2008 and 2011, its real-GDP growth rate exceeded the equal-weighted Wuhan–Qingdao benchmark by an average of 1.80 percentage points. In 2013–2015, the sign reversed: Dalian grew on average 2.77 percentage points more slowly than the same benchmark. The change in the relative gap is therefore approximately −4.57 percentage points.

The annual pattern is more informative than the before–after average. Dalian still grew by 9.0 per cent in 2013, compared with 10.0 per cent in both Wuhan and Qingdao. The divergence widened in 2014, when Dalian grew by 5.8 per cent against 9.7 per cent in Wuhan and 8.0 per cent in Qingdao, and widened again in 2015, when Dalian grew by 4.2 per cent against 8.8 and 8.1 per cent respectively. The main break is therefore concentrated in 2014–2015 rather than appearing as an immediate one-year collapse in 2012.

The result is not driven by a single comparison city. Using Wuhan alone produces a pre/post relative deterioration of about 4.22 percentage points; using Qingdao alone produces a deterioration of about 4.92 points. A linear extrapolation of the 2008–2011 pre-event relative trend also leaves a substantial negative residual in 2013–2015. These checks are reported in the supplementary material because the substantive point is stable: Dalian experienced an unusually sharp loss of growth momentum relative to external cities, especially from 2014 onward.

### 4.2 Most of the divergence appears at the regional level

The comparison with Liaoning changes the interpretation of this slowdown. In 2008–2011, Dalian grew on average 1.95 percentage points faster than Liaoning as a whole. In 2013–2015, it still grew about 0.50 percentage points faster. Its relative deterioration against the province was therefore approximately −1.45 percentage points—substantially smaller than the −4.57-point deterioration against Wuhan and Qingdao.

This does not mean that “Liaoning caused Dalian's slowdown.” Dalian is part of Liaoning, and the provincial aggregate is not an independent control. The comparison shows something more specific: much of the change that makes Dalian look exceptional against outside cities was also occurring elsewhere in its regional economic environment. In 2014, Dalian and Liaoning both reported real-GDP growth of 5.8 per cent, while the Wuhan–Qingdao average was about 8.9 per cent. In 2015, Dalian grew by 4.2 per cent and Liaoning by 3.0 per cent. Dalian was still outperforming the province even as both fell well behind the external benchmark.

The spatial pattern therefore suggests two layers of adjustment. The larger layer is a Liaoning-wide loss of growth momentum relative to outside cities. A smaller layer remains specific to Dalian. This distinction is important because the political-network argument is most plausible as an explanation for the second layer, whereas the first requires a mechanism operating across the regional economy.

### 4.3 The regional investment regime turns

Real-estate and fixed-asset investment provide that regional mechanism. In 2013, real-estate development investment was still expanding rapidly in both Dalian and Liaoning, by 22.5 and 18.2 per cent respectively. In 2014, both turned sharply negative: −16.4 per cent in Dalian and −17.8 per cent in Liaoning. The contrast with external cities is striking. Wuhan's real-estate investment still grew by 23.5 per cent in 2014, while Qingdao's grew by 6.6 per cent.

The similarity extends beyond growth rates. Real-estate development accounted for 26.4 per cent of Dalian's fixed-asset investment in 2013 and 26.0 per cent of Liaoning's; in 2014 the shares were 21.1 and 21.7 per cent. By 2015, fixed-asset investment had fallen by 32.7 per cent in Dalian and 27.8 per cent in Liaoning, while real-estate investment fell by roughly 37.2 and 32.9 per cent. Indexing 2013 investment levels to 100 leaves Dalian and Liaoning at approximately 70 and 71 for total fixed investment by 2015, and about 53 and 55 for real-estate investment.

**[Figure 2 about here: The regional investment regime turns down]**

Property is therefore important but not sufficient to explain the full collapse. Between 2013 and 2015, the decline in real-estate investment accounted for roughly 42 per cent of the fall in Dalian's total fixed-asset investment and about 40 per cent of Liaoning's. During the most severe 2014–2015 contraction, those ratios were only about one quarter. By 2015, the downturn had spread from property into a broader contraction in construction and industrial investment.

The combined GDP and investment evidence clarifies what is meant by a “regional shock” in this article. It is not a causal claim running from the provincial government to Dalian. It is a shared exposure to an investment-led growth regime that was weakening across Liaoning at roughly the same time. Dalian's post-2012 performance becomes easier to understand once this common regional transition is separated from the additional city-specific deterioration examined in the next section.

## 5. From Industrial Cash Flow to Credit Contraction

### 5.1 The aggregate slowdown was concentrated in capital-intensive activity

The 4.2 per cent GDP growth reported for Dalian in 2015 can obscure what was happening inside the local economy. The slowdown was not evenly distributed across sectors. In 2013, secondary and tertiary industry expanded at similar rates, 9.4 and 9.1 per cent respectively. By 2014, secondary-industry growth had fallen to 5.0 per cent while services still grew by 7.0 per cent. In 2015, secondary-industry growth almost stalled at 0.9 per cent, whereas tertiary industry continued to grow by 8.2 per cent.

The contribution of the two sectors to aggregate growth shifted just as sharply. Secondary industry accounted for 55.4 per cent of Dalian's GDP growth in 2013, 44.8 per cent in 2014 and only 10.6 per cent in 2015. The tertiary sector's contribution rose from 41.4 to 52.4 and then 85.5 per cent. Dalian's headline GDP number therefore combined two very different processes: continued service-sector expansion and a near-collapse in the incremental contribution of the more capital-intensive industrial and construction economy.

Liaoning displays the same broad sectoral split. Secondary-industry growth declined from 8.9 per cent in 2013 to 5.2 per cent in 2014 and −0.2 per cent in 2015, while tertiary-industry growth remained positive at 9.2, 7.2 and 7.1 per cent. This parallel reinforces the interpretation of the first layer of Dalian's slowdown as part of a broader regional industrial and investment transition rather than a city-wide demand collapse unique to Dalian.

### 5.2 Firm cash flow deteriorated before the financial system visibly weakened

For credit conditions, sectoral value added is less important than the cash flow of borrowers. Dalian's above-scale industrial sector still expanded by 10.2 per cent in 2013. Industrial firms' main-business revenue grew by 9.7 per cent and total profits by 26.1 per cent. The picture changed in 2014: above-scale industrial value added slowed to 4.3 per cent, while revenue fell by 4.9 per cent and profits by 4.6 per cent. By 2015, industrial value added was down 4.5 per cent, main-business revenue was down 27.4 per cent and profits were down 30.7 per cent. Construction output followed the same direction, shifting from +14.2 per cent in 2013 to −8.7 per cent in 2014 and −30.0 per cent in 2015.

This sequence provides the missing bridge between the regional investment downturn and local finance. Bank lenders were operating in an environment in which industrial sales, profits and project returns were deteriorating while property and construction activity weakened at the same time. Lower expected cash flow reduces debt-service capacity; falling project returns make new lending less attractive; and weaker asset values narrow the margin of safety behind collateralised lending.

The timing also matters. The sharp deterioration in industrial revenue and profit is visible in 2014, the same year in which Dalian's relative growth gap widens markedly. The financial indicators then continue to worsen. This is consistent with a feedback process in which the investment downturn weakens firm balance sheets, and deteriorating balance sheets subsequently make the financial system less willing to sustain marginal projects.

### 5.3 Credit conditions weakened more in Dalian than in Liaoning

Bank lending provides one of the clearest Dalian-specific divergences. In 2011–2012, Dalian's loan growth had broadly converged with Liaoning's. Dalian's loan growth was about 11.6 per cent in 2013 compared with 12.8 per cent for Liaoning, a gap of roughly 1.2 percentage points. The gap widened to about 3.4 points in 2014 and 3.3 points in 2015, and to more than 4 points by 2016.

The composition of new lending moved in the same direction. Dalian accounted for roughly 31.4 per cent of Liaoning's increment in bank loans in 2013, but only 23.4 per cent in 2014 and 21.9 per cent in 2015. At the same time, Dalian's non-performing-loan ratio rose from 1.07 per cent in 2013 to 1.86 per cent in 2014, 2.16 per cent in 2015 and 3.22 per cent in 2016.

**[Figure 4 about here: Credit weakening and asset-quality deterioration in Dalian]**

Private investment also weakened. In 2014, private fixed investment in Dalian grew by only 2.4 per cent, compared with 16.1 per cent in Wuhan and 24.0 per cent in Qingdao. This matters because it suggests that the local deterioration was not confined to government-led investment or bank balance sheets. Private investors were also becoming less willing to commit capital.

The financing data reveal a further distinction. Bank-loan increments weakened substantially, but direct financing through capital markets remained comparatively large. Dalian's annual increase in bank loans fell from RMB 75.55 billion in 2014 to RMB 70.99 billion in 2015 and RMB 26.75 billion in 2016, while reported direct capital-market financing remained high. Substantively, the pattern is clear: financing stress was segmented rather than universal. Continued direct-financing volumes show that at least some firms retained access to bond and capital-market channels even as bank credit and private investment weakened.

This segmentation is where the political-network mechanism becomes economically plausible. Political connections need not determine aggregate funding. Their value can be greater at the margin—when a project requires refinancing, when a bank is uncertain about local support, when collateral values are weakening, or when coordination across agencies determines whether an investment proceeds. The data do not identify which individual loan depended on political intervention, but they show that the Dalian-specific deterioration was concentrated precisely in the part of the economy where such coordination would be most valuable.

Taken together, the evidence suggests a two-stage process. The first stage was regional: property, fixed investment and industrial profitability weakened across Liaoning. The second stage was more local: Dalian's asset quality deteriorated and bank credit increasingly underperformed the province. If the repricing of political-network value mattered, these patterns place its most plausible role in amplifying an existing adjustment rather than creating the underlying regional downturn.

## 6. What Did Not Collapse: Public Resources and External Capital

If Dalian's slowdown resulted mainly from the sudden loss of high-level political access, one would expect visible public resources to weaken alongside local credit. The available evidence points in a different direction. Comparable central and provincial tax rebates and transfers to Dalian totalled RMB 18.897 billion in 2014 and RMB 19.810 billion in 2015, an increase of about 4.8 per cent in nominal terms. Earlier fiscal reports use different categories and are therefore not joined mechanically into the same series. The 2014–2015 comparison therefore cannot establish that fiscal support was unchanged from pre-2012 levels; it shows only that the observed budgetary channel did not collapse during the years in which Dalian's growth and bank credit weakened most sharply.

National policy platforms also continued to reach Dalian after 2012. The State Council approved the Jinpu New Area in 2014; Dalian entered the national cross-border e-commerce comprehensive pilot programme in 2016; and the Liaoning Free Trade Zone established a Dalian area in 2017. Large industrial investment did not disappear either. Intel announced another major investment in its Dalian facility in 2015. The city's top political leadership also showed substantial continuity through the initial slowdown: the municipal party secretary who took office in 2011 remained in position until 2017, while the mayor serving at the time of the 2012 event remained until the end of 2014.

**[Figure 5 about here: What did not collapse—formal public resources and policy platforms]**

These observations do not imply that political networks were irrelevant. They change the channel through which such networks are most likely to have mattered. Dalian continued to obtain formal fiscal resources, national policy designations and large projects at the same time that bank-credit growth, private investment and asset quality weakened. The contrast is more consistent with a deterioration in marginal coordination and financing conditions than with a comprehensive withdrawal of state support.

Dalian's unusually deep economic links with Japan provide another potential explanation for its slowdown. New Japanese-invested projects declined from 136 in 2012 to 75 in 2013, 61 in 2014 and 47 in 2015. This sharp fall in project entry suggests that one of the city's long-standing external growth channels was maturing. Yet the timing is less consistent with a sudden 2014 trigger. Actual Japanese investment in Dalian was about US$2.63 billion in 2013 and US$2.54 billion in 2014, a decline of only 3.5 per cent in the latter year. JETRO data indicate that Dalian remained a major concentration of Japanese investment in Liaoning even as new project formation slowed.

The Japanese evidence is therefore best understood as a slow-moving structural headwind. Fewer new entrants reduced the incremental contribution of an established external manufacturing network, but incumbent investment did not disappear at the moment when Dalian's GDP, industrial cash flow and bank credit began to diverge sharply. The 2015 investment figures show a much larger decline, although a change in statistical methodology limits direct comparison across that year.

Combining the public-resource and Japanese evidence narrows the interpretation further. There is no evidence of a contemporaneous collapse in the observed formal state-resource channels during the 2014–2015 slowdown, nor did Japanese capital suddenly vanish in 2014. The most immediate deterioration occurred in the domestic investment–industrial–credit chain. Formal public-resource channels and incumbent foreign investment were more resilient than bank credit and private investment. This asymmetry is central to the article's argument: political networks may be most economically important not when they deliver headline resources, but when they help sustain projects and credit relationships under worsening local conditions.

## 7. Chongqing and the Conditional Effect of Political Shocks

Chongqing is the most useful comparison for evaluating whether the 2012 political rupture should be expected to produce a broadly similar macroeconomic pattern elsewhere. Bo Xilai was serving as Chongqing party secretary when the crisis occurred, so the city was more directly exposed to the immediate political event than Dalian. Yet its post-2012 economic trajectory was very different.

Before the event, Chongqing already grew faster than an equal-weighted Chengdu–Wuhan benchmark. Its average relative growth advantage was about 1.55 percentage points in 2008–2011. In 2013–2016, that advantage did not disappear; it increased to roughly 2.35 percentage points. Chongqing's absolute GDP growth slowed as the national economy decelerated, but it did not experience the kind of relative reversal visible in Dalian.

The contrast is even clearer in investment and credit. Chongqing's bank-loan growth declined gradually from 20.0 per cent in 2011 to 14.6 per cent in 2014 and about 11.2 per cent in 2016, remaining in double digits throughout. Dalian's loan growth fell from 16.3 per cent in 2011 to 7.4 per cent in 2014 and 2.3 per cent in 2016. Fixed-asset investment in Chongqing still expanded by 18.0 per cent in 2014 and 17.1 per cent in 2015; Dalian recorded only 4.6 per cent growth in 2014 and a 32.7 per cent contraction in 2015. Chongqing's real-estate investment also remained positive through 2015, while Dalian and Liaoning had already turned sharply negative.

The point of this comparison is not that Chongqing constitutes a clean control for Dalian. The two cities differ in administrative status, urbanisation stage, industrial structure and fiscal position. Those differences are precisely what make the comparison informative for the article's theoretical claim. A common political rupture landed on two different local growth regimes. In Chongqing, investment opportunities, credit expansion and urban development remained relatively strong. In Dalian, the shock coincided with a regional property and industrial downturn and a subsequent deterioration in corporate cash flow and bank asset quality.

This divergence makes a mechanical political explanation difficult to sustain. This is not a same-treatment test: the intensity and nature of Dalian's historical exposure and Chongqing's contemporaneous exposure were different. Rather, Chongqing challenges the broader claim that an elite political rupture by itself is sufficient to produce a local investment and credit collapse. The evidence is more consistent with a conditional effect in which political networks matter most when economic conditions make marginal coordination and refinancing unusually valuable.

The comparison also clarifies what “local vulnerability” means in this article. It is not a fixed characteristic of a city. It is a state of the local growth regime. When investment returns are strong and financial institutions are still expanding credit, political disruption may be absorbed without a visible macroeconomic break. When returns, cash flow and collateral values are already weakening, the loss of coordinating capacity can reinforce retrenchment. The different post-2012 paths of Dalian and Chongqing therefore connect the case evidence to a broader argument about the state-dependent economic value of political connections.

## 8. Discussion and Limitations

The evidence changes the interpretation of Dalian's post-2012 slowdown in two important ways. First, it moves the centre of explanation away from a person-specific account. Dalian did experience a pronounced relative slowdown, but most of the external growth-gap reversal occurred alongside a wider Liaoning downturn in property, fixed investment and secondary industry. The political event remains relevant because it may have altered the city's ability to manage adjustment, not because it plausibly created the regional investment cycle.

Second, the results separate formal resource access from marginal coordination. Dalian continued to receive fiscal transfers, national policy designations and major industrial projects. At the same time, industrial cash flow deteriorated, non-performing loans rose, private investment weakened and bank-credit growth increasingly lagged the province. This divergence suggests that political connections may be economically valuable in ways that are not visible in headline fiscal or project totals. Their importance can lie in whether borderline projects obtain refinancing, whether banks expect local support, whether distressed firms can coordinate with public actors and whether private investors perceive policy backing as credible.

This distinction helps reconcile two strands of the Chinese political-economy literature. Research on local development emphasises the unusually active economic role of subnational governments (Oi 1992; Walder 1995; Xu 2011), while work on political selection and patronage shows that careers and resource allocation are shaped by political ties (Li and Zhou 2005; Jia, Kudamatsu, and Seim 2015; Persson and Zhuravskaya 2016; Jiang and Zhang 2020). The Dalian case suggests that the economic return to those ties depends on the condition of the local economy. Connections may be relatively unimportant when projects are highly profitable and finance is abundant, but more consequential when deteriorating cash flow and asset quality push projects toward the margin.

The article also places a limit on how much of Dalian's slowdown should be attributed to political mechanisms. The city-specific deterioration relative to Liaoning is on the order of one to two percentage points in the main windows, considerably smaller than the four-to-five-point deterioration relative to external cities. That city-specific component includes all Dalian-specific forces—not only political networks, but also industrial composition, Japanese exposure, borrower quality and other unobserved differences. The aggregate data therefore identify the scale on which a local amplification mechanism could operate, not a stand-alone political treatment effect.

Several limitations follow from the design. Liaoning contains Dalian in the provincial aggregate, so the nested comparison is a spatial decomposition rather than an independent control experiment. Wuhan and Qingdao are transparent external benchmarks but do not reproduce every pre-2012 characteristic of Dalian. Local statistical series are also affected by historical revisions, especially after economic censuses and changes in accounting practice. Liaoning additionally acknowledged that some cities and counties had fabricated fiscal-revenue data during 2011–2014 (Ministry of Finance 2017). That episode concerned fiscal data and does not by itself establish that the real-GDP growth series used here were fabricated, but it reinforces the need to treat provincial aggregates as contextual evidence rather than precise causal controls. For this reason, the article reports contemporaneous official growth rates in the main analysis, keeps the statistically unusual 2016 observations outside the principal regional window, and moves synthetic-control diagnostics based on mixed historical vintages to the supplementary material.

Most importantly, the proposed political channel is observed indirectly. The article has no direct index of Dalian's political-network exposure in 2012. Annual city data can show that formal public resources remained available while bank-dependent and private activity weakened; they cannot reveal which individual loans, projects or firms were affected by a change in political coordination. A stronger causal test would require firm–bank credit data, policy-bank lending, central-state-enterprise investment and measurable political-network exposure at the project or firm level. Such data would allow future research to test directly whether politically connected coordination becomes more valuable when local asset quality deteriorates.

Despite these limitations, the case supports a broader research agenda. Studies of political connections often estimate an average benefit of access to a patron or network. The evidence here suggests a different question: under what local economic conditions does losing such access become costly? Cross-city research could interact political-network shocks with ex ante property dependence, industrial profitability, local debt, bank asset quality or investment returns. That approach would move from asking whether political connections matter to explaining when and through which margins they shape economic adjustment.

## 9. Conclusion

Dalian's post-2012 slowdown is real, but its structure is different from the simplest political narrative. Relative to Wuhan and Qingdao, the city's real-GDP growth performance deteriorated by roughly 4.6 percentage points between the pre-event and post-event windows. Relative to Liaoning, however, the deterioration was much smaller. Real-estate and fixed-asset investment show that Dalian and the wider province were moving through a common investment and industrial downturn.

Disaggregating GDP clarifies how that regional shift reached local finance. Secondary-industry growth nearly stalled while services remained comparatively resilient; industrial revenue, profits and construction output then fell sharply. Non-performing loans rose and bank-credit growth weakened relative to Liaoning. The key local mechanism was therefore not simply slower aggregate growth, but a deterioration in the cash flow and asset quality of the borrowers most dependent on investment and bank finance.

At the same time, the observed formal public-resource channels did not collapse during the key 2014–2015 slowdown. Fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and major projects continued, and Chongqing did not reproduce Dalian's post-2012 credit and investment collapse despite being more directly exposed to the 2012 political rupture. These differences point to a conditional interpretation of political networks. Their macroeconomic value depends on the economic environment in which they operate: when investment returns and balance sheets are healthy, a political shock may be absorbed; when the local growth regime is already weakening, changes in coordination, refinancing and risk expectations can amplify the adjustment.

For Dalian, the 2012 political shock may therefore have changed the friction of the turn rather than the direction of the turn itself. The regional investment cycle set the main downward trajectory; Dalian's industrial and financial structure shaped its additional exposure; and political networks, together with credit feedback, may have influenced the magnitude of adjustment. This state-dependent view provides a more general explanation of why political connections can matter greatly in some local economic settings and leave much smaller macroeconomic traces in others.
