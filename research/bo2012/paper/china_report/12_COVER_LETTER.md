Dear Editor,

Please consider the manuscript, **“Political Network Shocks and Local Economic Vulnerability: Evidence from Dalian after 2012,”** for publication as a Research Article in *China Report*.

The article examines a broader question in Chinese local political economy: why does the loss of political-network value produce a visible economic break in some localities but not in others? It uses Dalian as the primary case, places the city's post-2012 slowdown within the wider Liaoning investment and industrial downturn, and traces a mechanism from weakening industrial cash flow to deteriorating asset quality and bank credit. The article also compares Dalian with Chongqing, which was more directly exposed to the same 2012 political rupture but did not experience a comparable investment or credit collapse.

The main finding is that political-network shocks do not translate mechanically into local economic decline. Dalian's largest growth deterioration coincided with a broader Liaoning property and fixed-investment downturn, while the more city-specific weakness appeared in bank credit, non-performing loans and private investment. At the same time, fiscal transfers, national policy platforms and major projects continued. The evidence therefore suggests that political networks are most likely to affect adjustment through marginal financing, project coordination and risk expectations when the underlying local growth regime is already vulnerable.

We believe the manuscript fits *China Report* because it addresses political institutions, subnational economic development and the interaction between political networks and local financial conditions in contemporary China. Its contribution is a mechanism-based political-economy interpretation rather than a claim to a single precisely estimated treatment effect.
